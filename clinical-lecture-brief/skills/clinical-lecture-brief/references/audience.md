# Audience calibration

The same lecture becomes a different document for each reader. Fix the audience before
writing, and let it drive four things: **depth, vocabulary, emphasis, and the practice
section.**

## The dial

| Audience | Assume they know | Depth | Emphasise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-clinical student | Physiology, little clinical context | Mechanism first, then application | Why the mechanism produces the sign |
| Medical student / intern | Physiology and pathophysiology; limited independent decisions | Full mechanism **and** full workup | Differential reasoning, why each test discriminates, what result changes what |
| APN / nursing intern | Strong bedside assessment and patient management; less pathophysiology | Algorithm first, mechanism as support | What to order next, monitoring, escalation triggers, patient education |
| Resident / registrar | Both, plus prior exposure | Compressed fundamentals, expanded edge cases | Where guidelines conflict, mixed presentations, management thresholds |
| Pharmacy | Drug handling and interactions | Mechanism at the drug level | Agent-specific detail, dosing, monitoring, interactions |

**Mixed backgrounds** are common and are a real instruction, not a contradiction.
"Medical student with an APN background" means: pitch the reasoning at medical-student
depth, but you may assume bedside competence, and keep the practical ordering and
escalation content an APN would already value. State the read you took, so the user can
correct it.

## What changes concretely

**Depth.** A student needs the mechanism that makes a finding discriminating. A
practitioner needs the threshold and the next action. Most audiences need both, in
different proportions - shift the ratio, do not delete either.

**Vocabulary.** Expand every abbreviation on first use regardless of audience. Seniority
buys compression of explanation, not of definition.

**Emphasis.** The same table serves different questions. For a student, the discriminating
column is the lesson. For a practitioner, the action column is.

**Practice section.** Always include one, always specific to the role:

- Escalation triggers - what must not wait, and who to call
- Ordering pitfalls - tests invalidated by timing, treatment, or physiological state
- Sequencing errors that cause harm
- Patient education, where the role owns that conversation

Generic safety advice is filler. "Check B12 before replacing folate, because folate
corrects the count while the neurological damage progresses" earns its place. "Always
follow local protocols" does not, beyond a single line in the footer.

## Scope discipline

Do not silently widen scope to the whole specialty, and do not narrow a lecture that
covered a full algorithm into a summary of its first third. Cover what the lecture
covered, at the depth the audience needs.

Where the lecture omits something the audience genuinely requires, add it and **say you
added it**. That is the difference between a useful reference and an unreliable one.
